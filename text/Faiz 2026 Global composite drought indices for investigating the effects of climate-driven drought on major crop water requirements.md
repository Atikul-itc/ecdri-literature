**Abstract** Droughts are expected to increase in warmer climates, significantly affecting global crop production and raising crop water demand. The recurrence of extreme droughts in recent years needs more attention to capture their multifaceted nature. Explaining the impact of general drought indices on water requirements could limit the precision of future water demand estimates. Three composite drought indices were used to investigate the effects of drought on crop water requirement ( _CWR_ ), irrigation water requirement ( _IWR_ ), and yields for major crops under the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs 245 and 585), to provide a broader view of future water-demand risks. The results indicate that drought distribution is accompanied by wetness, with a higher likelihood of future dryness in regions such as South Asia, Australia, and North and South America. There is an increase in the estimates of the _CWRs_ and _IWRs_ under the SSPs. In extreme cases (SSP 585), maximum fractional changes in total (including rainfed and irrigated) _CWR_ and _IWR_ relative to the baseline period are +80.16 mm/month and +42.68 mm/month for wheat; +52.54 mm/month and +7.09 mm/month for maize; and +14.01 mm/month and +4.98 mm/month for rice, respectively. Overall, the results indicate a significant increase in _CWR_ and _IWR_ under future climate scenarios, underscoring the potential for increased water demand and the urgent need to develop adaptive water management strategies. 

**Keywords** Drought, Evapotranspiration, Major crops, Monitoring, CMIP6 

## **1. Introduction** 

Growing evidence indicates the intensification of climate extremes (Xu et al., 2019; Seneviratne et al., 2021), especially the land area at risk from drought under projected climate models (Harrison et al., 2014). Drought is the most influential and widespread natural disaster and a significant threat to agricultural crop production (Liu et al., 2016). Agriculture is the primary source of income in many countries, and approximately 1.3 billion people rely on it; this sector is highly affected by drought (Pourzand and Noy, 2022). In the last two decades, many countries have experienced substantial drought losses (Attiwill and Adams, 2013; Basara et al., 2019). A large portion of freshwater is used by agriculture, and the availability of water resources is at risk because of rapid population growth and climate change (Zhang et al., 2023). Increases in temperature under climate change accelerate global hydrological cycles and cause variations in the spatial distribution of global droughts (Naumann et al., 2018). Thus, global spatial dryness and its effect on agricultural crop water requirements via updated drought indices are crucial for mitigating adverse effects on food production, agriculture, and natural resources. 

Maize, rice, and wheat are essential cereals that guarantee feed and food security worldwide (Shiferaw et al., 2013; Challinor et al., 2014). However, their growth and productivity are tied to meteorological factors like drought stress. Precipitation is a key climate variable that mostly meets crop water requirement ( _CWR_ ), and crop yield production is vulnerable to drought due to the variability of effective precipitation and high temperatures (Naresh Kumar et al., 2014; Wang J et al., 2018a). The increasing risk of drought and the decrease in regional projected precipitation may further cause a decline in the production of these cereals (O’Leary et al., 2018; Wang J et al., 2018b). Water stress triggers yield loss if irrigation water shortages occur at any crop growth stage (Anjum et al., 2017; Dietz et al., 2021). Optimizing irrigation schemes and extending the irrigation network could help mitigate the adverse impacts of drought on crop production. However, only 25% of the area is irrigated worldwide based on MIRCA2000, and a portion of dominant crops, such as maize (0.3 million km<sup>2</sup> ), wheat (0.7 million km<sup>2</sup> ), and rice (1.0 million km<sup>2</sup> ) is harvested in irrigated areas. Maize and wheat are largely grown in 1.2 million km<sup>2</sup> to 1.5 million km<sup>2</sup> rainfed harvest areas (Portmann et al., 2010). Seasonal drought can delay the sowing of crops, influencing grain yield

(Dietz et al., 2021). Thus, it is necessary to understand the _CWR_ and irrigation water requirement ( _IWR_ ) distributions during drought to better manage water shortages and design irrigation schemes (Kraemer, 2015; Boonwichai et al., 2018). 

Several drought indices have been developed to monitor and quantify drought at temporal and spatial scales (Dai, 2011; Zargar et al., 2011; Faiz et al., 2021). Among these, the Standardized Precipitation Index ( _SPI_ ), Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index ( _SPEI_ ), Palmer Drought Severity Index ( _PDSI_ ), Crop Moisture Deficit Index ( _CMDI_ ), and terrestrial water storage metrics (integration of snow, groundwater, and soil moisture deficit) are used for drought characterization because they effectively represent different drought types (Palmer, 1965; McKee et al., 1993; Narasimhan and Srinivasan, 2005; Vicente-Serrano et al., 2010; Pokhrel et al., 2021; Yin et al., 2022). Likewise, researchers developed composite and multivariate drought indices to track the progression from meteorological deficits to soil-moisture stress and their impacts. For example, the composite/integrated or combined drought indicators, which integrate precipitation, soil-moisture anomaly, and vegetation stress to monitor and alert to the drought and its impact on reduced plant activity (Sepulcre-Canto et al., 2012). The Multivariate Standardized Drought Index ( _MSDI_ ) pairs precipitation with soil moisture index (Hao and AghaKouchak 2013), while the Joint Deficit Index ( _JDI_ ) uses a copula framework to link joint precipitation and streamflow deficits to drought probabilities (Kao and Govindaraju, 2010) and Integrated Drought Condition Index ( _IDCI_ ) used meteorological drought index, vegetation conditions and soil moisture to assess the agricultural drought hazards (Shen et al., 2019). These indices collectively enhance our understanding of drought dynamics by providing insights into the severity, duration, and spatial extent of drought events. They are widely used but have limitations due to their methodological differences and varying effectiveness in different regions. A drought index should be statistically robust and capable of comparing performance across diverse areas (Keyantash and Dracup, 2002). To address these limitations, composite drought indices that combine various hydro-climatic factors have been developed, such as the Composite Drought Index ( _CDI_ ) (Faiz et al., 2022). The _CDI_ integrates a modified rainfall anomaly index ( _RAI m_ ), a waterbalance anomaly index ( _WBAI_ ), and a moisture index ( _MI_ , calculated from precipitation and actual evapotranspiration). By considering rainfall deficits, water balance, and evaporative demand, the _CDI_ provides a comprehensive view of drought processes. During its development, the _CDI_ was tested against soil moisture, streamflow, and scaled crop yield indices across China, showing strong correlations with agricultural impacts and fewer false alarms compared to _PDSI_ and evaluation integrated drought index ( _EIDI_ ) (Faiz et al., 2022). Recently, _CDI_ has been enhanced with different evapotranspiration formulation datasets and machine learning techniques, further aligning it with soil moisture and crop water requirements (Faiz et al., 2025). These results showed that the _CDI_ is both scientifically robust for assessing agricultural water stress. However, our goal is not to suggest that the _CDI_ is universally better or that it replaces existing drought monitoring systems. Instead, we use the _CDI_ as a tool to generate a consistent drought signal that can be directly connected to _CWR_ and _IWR_ under future climate scenarios. Although numerous multivariate indices exist (Hao and AghaKouchak, 2013; Zargar et al., 2011), few studies have directly 

linked these indices to agricultural water demand and irrigation planning (Wada et al., 2013; Nechifor and Winning, 2019). The novelty of our work lies in combining the three sets of _CDIs_ with _CWR_ / _IWR_ estimates and multi-model climate projections, enabling us to measure how changing drought patterns will impact global crop water demands. This approach connects drought diagnosis with resource management. Moreover, many studies have reported that drought severity may increase under a warming climate, as reflected by increased stress on water resources and vegetation, increased drought risk, crop _IWRs_ , and _CWRs_ (Murray et al., 2012; Wang Z et al., 2018; VicenteSerrano et al., 2020). Droughts substantially affect crop yields and agricultural productivity by altering _CWR_ and _IWR_ , resulting in lower agricultural output and economic losses (Lobell et al., 2011; Daryanto et al., 2017; Leng and Hall, 2019). Thus, applying the _CDIs_ is crucial for understanding the effects of drought on _IWR_ and _CWR_ , as well as the future implications for agricultural water management, irrigation scheduling, and irrigation infrastructure planning. In other words, it enhances the ability to improve crop productivity and water use efficiency. 

This study aims to examine the relationships between drought and _CWR_ and _IWR_ and provide a context for water requirements at a global scale to inform irrigation water resource decisionmaking. Particularly, we set up the following objectives: (1) To analyze the spatial distribution of drought conditions and their relationships with _CWR_ and _IWR_ ; (2) To project future changes in drought, _CWR_ , and _IWR_ under multiple climate scenarios; (3) To assess the _CWR_ and _IWR_ for historical periods and future scenarios in different cropping areas.

## **2.2 Composite drought index (CDI)** 

The _CDI_ is a composite of the _WBAI_ , _RAI m_ , and _MI_ (Faiz et al., 2022). For the _WBAI_ and _RAI m_ , the precipitation total and median water balance, which is based on the difference between precipitation and _AE_ / _PET_ (in this case), were used as the average distribution, and average wet and dry extreme cases were used to define the distribution variability (Hänsel et al., 2019). _RAI m_ captures the precipitation-driven meteorological signal; _WBAI_ adds evaporative demand via the difference between precipitation and _AE_ / _PET_ , which is closely related to water-balance deficits; _MI_ converts conditions to a moisture ratio linked to soil moisture and crop water stress. ## **2.4 Random forest regression** 

Random Forest Regression (RFR) can be used to analyze interactions among variables and manage nonlinear relationships (Li et al., 2023). RFR builds multiple decision trees instead of a single one to reduce overfitting during training. It was used to predict _CWR_ and _IWR_ based on features ( _CDI_Main_ , _CDI_W_ , _CDI_NCC_ , month, and year) for selected river basins. The random forest was built with 500–1000 trees. We experimented with different settings and the number of features. Cross-validation was employed to ensure the model’s generalizability. The model used bootstrapping, and to avoid overestimating accuracy, a rolling origin time split (80:20 ratio) was applied rather than random train-test splits. All data preparation steps, such as lagging and transformations, were performed within the training folds and then applied to the test folds. Model performance was assessed using multiple metrics, including mean squared error (MSE) and the coefficient of determination. Additionally, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values were calculated to understand the contribution of features (Gebreyesus et al., 2023) to the model’s predictions. SHAP decomposes each prediction to reveal how individual features influence the predicted _CWR_ and _IWR_ . Feature importance across all test folds was summarized, and the effects of _CDI_ and its lags on _CWR_ and _IWR_ , including how their effects vary with seasonality, were examined.

Trade map. URL: https://www. trademap. org (Access Date: 3/3/ 2023)). The average monthly _CWR_ were estimated for wheat (11.08 mm (low) to 41.80 mm (severe)), rice (35.89 mm (low) to 121.29 mm (severe)), and maize (19.36 mm (low) to 71.55 mm (severe)) global cropland areas. South China, North and South America, Africa, India, Southeast Asia, and Australia’s northeast and southeast coasts presented higher _CWR_ . Similarly, the average monthly estimated _IWR_ were 0.62 mm to 7.10 mm for wheat, 7.55 mm to 34.92 mm for rice, and 2.75 mm to 14.80 mm for maize. The spatial distribution of the _IWR_ area was lower than that of the _CWR_ area. _IWR_ was high in Pakistan, some parts of North America, South America, Africa, and Australia (Fig. 1). High and severe-dominated _CWR_ and _IWR_ clusters could be observed in significant cash crop countries. 

Given the importance of cash crops, the composite indices were correlated with crop yields (Fig. 2 and Appendices S2 and S2.1). The indices showed a significant relationship with different crop yields. Wheat and maize yields showed a positive and significant correlation at the 0.05 level in North America, and a positive but nonsignificant correlation in European countries. In contrast, China, Australia, and South Asian countries dominated the mixed scenario (significant and non-significant relationships with drought indices). Rice had a negative correlation in most countries but a positive correlation in African countries. Rice is an irrigated and rainfed crop; thus, future droughts could reduce the yield of rainfed crops. The results indicate that composite drought indices exhibit stronger positive correlations with crop yields in major agricultural regions. However, negative correlation or lower correlation values were obtained in some areas, indicating that other factors also affect yield besides water availability in drought conditions. Findings revealed that composite drought indices could support a comprehensive drought impact assessment framework by integrating information that connects meteorological drought monitoring with hydrological and agricultural impacts. This could help with water resource planning and drought risk management. 

The seasonal drought patterns and the positive relationship between drought and yield indicate that crop water requirements can be high during drought periods. _CWR_ shows a positive correlation in most crop-producing countries. Among the drought indices, _CDI_W_ , which assigns fixed weights to rainfall anomaly, water balance anomaly, and moisture index, exhibits stronger correlations (>0.5) in Australia, India, South America, and African countries. ## **3.2 Projection under future change scenarios** 

We evaluated the composite drought indices for the spring and summer seasons under two SSPs (245 and 585) and nine models (described in the dataset and supporting information as supplementary material). Plots of dryness/wetness are presented in Appendix Figs. S9–S14. The climate models under both SSPs presented similar average dryness and wetness values from 2024 to 2060 and from 2061 to 2099, respectively. The first phase (2024 to 2060) had less dryness and more wetness, whereas the second phase (2061 to 2099) inferred more dryness worldwide. For example, North America, South America, Australia, Europe, and Asia presented high degrees of dryness. From 2023 to 2060, Asian countries, including North China, India, and Pakistan, experienced dryness in the spring season, while parts of Europe and Canada exhibited consistent wetness. 

We also explored the composite drought index distributions under the various SSPs in the future to assess wetness and dryness in different basins. The average calculated indices were modeled for ten different river basins spanning the historical period (2000 to 2021) to the end of the century (2024 to 2099) under nine GCMs (Fig. 4 and Appendix Figs. S15 and S16). The variations in wetness and dryness can be seen as slightly greater than those historically reported. Under the SSP scenarios, the _CDI_ distribution exhibited a slight negative skew, suggesting the potential for both dry and wet conditions in the future, with a tendency toward wetter conditions in certain regions. The distribution’s peak was consistently near zero across all basins, pointing to dominant near-normal conditions compared to the historical baseline. In several basins, such as the Mississippi, Yellow, and Yangtze, there was evidence of a broader range of positive _CDI_ values, implying a higher likelihood of wetter conditions ahead. The peak around zero needs careful interpretation. This pattern may partly stem from methodological factors, such as the limited historical reference period used in this study, which may restrict the ability to fully capture extreme drought and wet events. It also results from calculating _CDIs_ values at the grid-cell level before aggregating over large basins. These aspects add some uncertainty to the distribution’s shape and central tendency. However, the consistent trend of distributions broadening toward positive _CDIs_ values across multiple models and scenarios suggests that the anticipated increase in wetness is not merely due to methodological or dataset biases but is a robust finding from ensemble projections. This underscores the need to account for both methodological uncertainty and multi-model agreement when analyzing future drought and wetness trends (a detailed explanation is provided in Appendix S2.2). Furthermore, it is worth noting that the different _CDI_ variants ( _CDI_ _W, _CDI_NCC_ , and _CDI_Main_ ) exhibited somewhat different dryness and wetness distributions. This variation reflects their different sensitivities to the calculation or formulation used to calculate the moisture index. These differences highlight that future drought assessments depend on the index used, underscoring the need to interpret results within the broader, multidimensional context of drought processes.

## **4. Discussion and summary** 

We used composite indices to demonstrate the effects of wetness and dryness on _CWR_ , _IWR_ , and crop yields across different basins and global scales. For a robust analysis, the evaluated datasets were employed to account for the contributions of climate warming. _SPI_ and _SPEI_ were used for comparison purposes, and the _SPEI_ calculation was made using precipitation and GLEAM actual evaporation data. The results reaffirm the _CDI_ ’s ability to represent drought conditions across regions worldwide effectively. However, it is essential to note that the previous study (Faiz et al., 2022) was validated using only one region. The differences observed between _CDI_ , _SPI_ , and _SPEI_ can be explained because _CDIs_ incorporates multiple variables (precipitation, evapotranspiration, and climatic moisture deficit), making it comparable to capturing and evaluating drought effects on agricultural systems. Although _SPI_ and _SPEI_ are useful for detecting precipitation and water balance anomalies, our case study comparisons show that _CDIs_ can reveal additional spatiotemporal differences, especially in climate transition zones or semi-arid areas. Overall, the results of _CDIs_ demonstrate that CDIs can enhance water management and agricultural productivity under changing climate conditions. This is especially important in rain-fed agricultural regions that are highly sensitive to drought characterization for ensuring food security. Moreover, the choice of data can lead to different estimates of drought characteristics (Beguería et al., 2014), which in turn influences assessments of agricultural vulnerability and food security. Previous studies (Zhao et al., 2017; Solaraju-Murali et al., 2021) have demonstrated that drought and heat stress significantly reduce crop yields at both global and regional levels. By integrating multiple drought drivers, the _CDI_ framework could be better equipped to capture such combined stresses, thereby offering reliable insights into agricultural risks under future climate scenarios. Composite indices represented well the European summer drought in 2018, as recorded by previous studies (Blauhut et al., 2022; Rakovec et al., 2022). An increase in the _CWR_ and _IWR_ of selected crops was dominant in some regions and may affect food security. Anthropogenic influences have greater effects on precipitation variability, such as in Europe (Christidis and Stott, 2021), which could exacerbate this phenomenon. 

The results provide vital features of _CWR_ and _IWR_ changes and the effects of drought on crop yield (similar findings reported by (Marcos-Garcia et al., 2024) that crop risk is associated with dry spells at the regional level). We found a 30% to 75% increase in _CWR_ at a regional level, which could be compensated by irrigation; regionally, up to 50% of yield could be lost under severe drought (Zwart and Bastiaanssen, 2004). Although the _CDIs_ , _IWR_ / _CWR_ , are statistical in nature, they aim to represent biophysical processes that restrict crop growth and productivity. Drought indices track the imbalance between water supply and atmospheric moisture demand. When soil water is scarce, stomatal closure cuts back transpiration and CO 2 uptake, which directly limits photosynthesis and biomass production (Lobell and Gourdji, 2012; Zhang and Cai, 2013). Soil moisture deficits and rooting depth affect crop resilience. Reduced rainfall and increased evaporative demand lower soil moisture, especially in the root zone. Crops with shallow roots (e.g., rice, wheat) are more susceptible to short-term soil water shortages. In contrast, deep-rooted crops (e.g., maize) can temporarily endure stress but are vulnerable under prolonged deficits (Sinclair and Muchow, 2001). Phenological sensitivity affects yields, particularly when drought occurs during key growth stages, such as flowering and grain filling, resulting in incomplete pollination, lower grain set, and a reduced harvest index (Barnabás et al., 2008). In irrigated systems, _IWR_ increases during drought. If the water supply cannot meet this increased demand due to surface water shortages or groundwater depletion, yield losses happen despite irrigation efforts (Siebert and Döll, 2010). We also acknowledge that _CWR_ and _IWR_ estimation on a larger scale would be a helpful extension of our research. The results could be promising, as we considered only composite indices for future estimations. For reliable prediction or variation estimation, a large ensemble is needed (Smith et al., 2019). To achieve this, heat stress, excess water, and climate oscillations could be examined in relation to yield, _CWR_ , and _IWR_ variations. The results of this study can be considered a first step in the development of climate services in crop regions. Simple composite indices could be used to support and provide drought information to farmers and crop breeders. They can benefit from drought information when deciding crop diversification and drought tolerance for their breeding programs. Overall, the present findings identify the need to take action to better manage agricultural water use.
