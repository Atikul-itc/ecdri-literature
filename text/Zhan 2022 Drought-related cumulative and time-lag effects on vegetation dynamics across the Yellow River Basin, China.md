#### **1. Introduction** 

As the principal component of terrestrial ecosystems, vegetation is vital for regulating climate and maintaining the water–carbon balance (Liu et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2019). It is commonly applied as an indicator of variations in ecosystems, because vegetation dynamics are vulnerable to climate change, particularly extreme climate events (Luo et al., 2020; Ying et al., 2020). Recent decades have witnessed an increase in the frequency and intensity of extreme climate, such as droughts, floods, and cold waves (AghaKouchak et al., 2020; Bellprat et al., 2019; 

Ummenhofer and Meehl, 2017). Of these phenomena, the most common is drought, which is a condition of prolonged and significantly lower water availability than the normal level, thereby causing a failure to supply water demand (Vicente-Serrano et al., 2013). Water availability is intimately related to vegetation growth, and chronically low water supplies may adversely affect vegetation function, even resulting in vegetation death (Gao et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2021). Additionally, drought substantially affects vegetation at various spatial and temporal scales because of its high frequency, long duration, widespread effects, and multiple timescales (Wang et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2017). above issues point to the need to investigate the association between vegetation dynamics and drought to facilitate the understanding of vegetation sensitivity to and drought tolerance (Anderegg et al., 2020; Fang and Xiong, 2015). 

Given that the spatiotemporal patterns of vegetation–drought interactions differ significantly depending on vegetation type, climate condition, and timescale, gaining a comprehensive grasp of how drought affects vegetation is difficult (Li et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2017). Among these variations, those in the timescales of drought that affect vegetation are one of the most significant mechanisms of climate–vegetation interactions (Jiang et al., 2020; Qi et al., 2022). Although several studies have focused on the response of vegetation to simultaneous or singletimescale droughts (Jiang et al., 2018; Mohammat et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2019), none of these explorations considered the extensive acknowledgment of vegetation as affected by the temporal effects of drought, including cumulative and time-lag effects (Hua et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2022; Zuo et al., 2021). Accordingly, identifying the temporal variability of vegetation responses to drought is crucial to expanding the knowledge of the mechanisms that underlie climate–vegetation interactions and developing effective vegetation conservation strategies. 

Time-lag effects describe how previous droughts affect current vegetation at a specific timescale, thus reflecting the drought sensitivity of vegetation (D’Orangeville et al., 2018; Wei et al., 2022a). Studies have extensively investigated the effects of lagged drought on vegetation growth, including Peng et al. (2019), who found that 46.2 % of the vegetated areas over the extratropical ecosystems in the Northern Hemisphere reflect the impact of lagged drought on autumn leaf senescence. The authors further discovered that in which the lagged months periods occurred mainly within six months. In Nebraska, Hua et al. (2019) found that vegetation typically responded in a lagged manner to drought within 30 to 45 days and that this response differed depending on vegetation type. Zhao et al. (2020) reported that nearly half of the grassland on the Loess Plateau continued to exhibit severe effects from a drought event that had occurred three months before. A similar legacy effect was observed by Xu et al. (2021), who indicated that 25 % of the vegetated area in northern China exhibited a predominant lag in effects of 9.1 months. 

Studies have also identified that a long-term deficiency in atmospheric and soil water availability (cumulative drought) significantly affects vegetation growth, inhibiting photosynthesis, reducing vegetation productivity, and even causing vegetation death (Wei et al., 2022a; Xu et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022). Cumulative drought refers to the effects of a period (spanning several months or even years) of water deficits on vegetation growth and can be used to assess vegetation tolerance to drought (Zhang et al., 2022). Peng et al. (2019) uncovered cumulative drought effects on 27.2 % of vegetated areas over the Northern Hemisphere at the end of the growing season, with the corresponding period of accumulated effects being one to four months. Zhao et al. (2020) reported that approximately 81 % of grasslands on the Loess Plateau are affected by cumulative drought, with accumulated effects occurring primarily from May to October. Wei et al. (2022a) revealed that 78.5 % of global grasslands are affected by cumulative drought and that the period of accumulated effects is longer in arid zones (eight–10 months) than in humid regions (less than six months). As indicated by Zhang et al. (2022), cumulative droughts have a wide range of effects on global vegetation, with a typical cumulative effect duration (CED) of 4.89 months. The authors also stated that CED is spatially and temporally heterogeneous across vegetation types, water balance levels, and elevation levels, among other factors. 

As a climate-sensitive area, the Yellow River Basin (YRB) in China covers arid, semi-arid, and semi-humid zones, with most areas receiving _<_ 500 mm of annual precipitation (Zhan et al., 2022). The YRB is one of the most severely eroded areas in the world, with vegetation in the basin vulnerable to multiscale droughts (Qin et al., 2022). Several studies have assessed the correlation between vegetation and simultaneous drought 

in the YRB, leading to a bias in understanding vegetation sensitivity and tolerance to multiscale drought (Geng et al., 2022; Jia et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2015; Zhao et al., 2018). Some studies have evaluated the temporal effects of drought on vegetation on the basis of either a single species or an entire community of vegetation (Jiang et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2022a; Zhao et al., 2020), which ignored the variability of vegetation responses to drought across climate zones and vegetation types. 

To bridge the above-mentioned gap, we used the long-term Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and the lagged and accumulated Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI) series of the YRB to (1) determine spatiotemporal patterns typifying the cumulative and time-lag effects of drought on vegetation dynamics for various vegetation types and climatic conditions and (2) identify the relationship between the intensity and duration of accumulated (lagged) drought effects on vegetation and annual water availability. 

#### **2. Materials and methods** 

#### _2.1. Study area_ 

The YRB originates in the Bayan Har Mountains of the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau and flows through nine provinces (autonomous regions) in China, covering an area of 79.5 × 10<sup>4</sup> km<sup>2</sup> (Ji et al., 2018). Its topography decreases from east to west and sequentially spans four geomorphological units: the Tibetan Plateau, Inner Mongolia Plateau, Loess Plateau, and Huang-Huai-Hai Plain. According to the ecogeographical classification of China developed by Zheng (1999), the basin can be divided into three climatic zones, being arid in the northwest, semi-arid in the center, and semi-humid in the southeast. Approximately 75 % of the vegetated area in the YRB consists of cultivated lands, grasslands, and meadows. Cultivated lands are predominantly located in the southeastern semi-arid zone and semi-humid zone, grasslands are found in the arid zone and northeastern semi-arid zone, and meadows are situated on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau (Fig. 1). 

#### _2.2. Data_ 

#### _2.2.1. NDVI dataset_ 

The NDVI3g.v1 dataset released by the Global Inventory Monitoring and Modeling System (GIMMS, https://ecocast.arc.nasa.gov/data/pub/ gimm s/3g.v1/), was utilized in this study. The dataset has a spatial resolution of 8 km × 8 km with a 15-day interval and covers the period 1982 to 2015. To eliminate the effects of clouds, aerosols, and other nonvegetation factors on remote sensing for monitoring, we calculated the monthly NDVI by the maximum value composite approach. To mitigate the effects of soil background on the NDVI in areas with low vegetation cover, pixels with multi-year average NDVI values _<_ 0.1 were considered unvegetated regions (Jin et al., 2022). 

#### _2.2.2. Drought index_ 

We obtained the monthly SPEI with a spatial resolution of 0.5<sup>◦</sup> at one- to 12-month timescales during 1981–2015 from the SPEIbase (version 2.6) of Consejo Superior de Investigaciones Científicas (https: //spei.csic.es/spei_database/). The SPEI reflects the moisture surplus or deficit divided by the monthly moisture deficit [precipitation minus potential evapotranspiration (PET)], where PET is calculated using the FAO-56 Penman–Monteith equation (Allen et al., 1998; Vicente-Serrano et al., 2010). Assuming that the cumulative moisture deficit in a single month of historical data follows a log-logistic distribution, the probability density function and cumulative probability are calculated and transformed into a standard normal distribution to obtain a multiscale SPEI. The one-month and 12-month SPEI timescales (e.g., in December), for example, are indicators of the atmospheric water balance during the current month and entire year (accumulated from one to 12 months), respectively (Vicente-Serrano et al., 2013). #### _2.3.1. Cumulative effects of drought on vegetation_ 

As illustrated in Fig. 2, We used the Pearson correlation coefficient ( _R_ ), we can determine whether a negative ( _R <_ 0) or positive ( _R >_ 0) relationship exists between drought and vegetation growth, with a higher value of _R_ indicating a more significant impact (Zhao et al., 2020). In Equations (1) and (2), _R culj_ is the Pearson coefficient of correlation between the NDVI and accumulated SPEI, _j_ is the timescale of accumulated effects from one- to 12- months, and _R_ max cul<sup>denotes the</sup> maximum absolute value of _R culj_ . 

#### _2.3.2. Lagged effects of drought on vegetation_ 

In contrast to the examination of the cumulative effects, for which one- to 12-month timescale SPEI was used, the lag effects involved a consideration of only the one-month timescale SPEI. We calculated correlation coefficients ( _R lagi_ , statistical significance was determined at _p <_ 0.05) with different lagged SPEI series [Equation (3)], and the lagged effect was determined at this pixel by considering the correlation coefficient between NDVI and accumulated SPEI that was significant. Then we determined the optimal lagged months and the corresponding intensity of the lagged effects for each pixel with the maximum _R lagi_ (i. e., _R_ max lag<sup>at</sup><sup>_p <_0.05) [Equation (4)].</sup> 





In the equations above, _R lagi_ is the Pearson coefficient of correlation between the NDVI and lagged SPEI series, SPEI _i_ denotes the one-month SPEI timescale at lagged _i_ month. The valuesof _i_ ranges from 0 to 12, where _i_ = 0 indicates no time-lag effect, while others indicate a time-lag of one- to 12- month. For example, a lag of one month was primarily analysed using the monthly SPEI from December 1981 to November 2015 correlated with the monthly NDVI over 1982–2015, and similarly to a lag of other lagged months (Fig. 2). _R_ max lag<sup>represents the maximum</sup> value of _R lagi_ , with the month in which _R_ max lag<sup>was recorded was</sup> considered the optimal lagged month. 

#### _2.3.3. Influence of hydrological conditions on cumulative and lagged effects_ 

Since SPEI was calculated based on the difference between monthly precipitation and PET, it is capable of reflecting the water balance and is primarily responsible for the observable differences in vegetation 

resistance to drought events and post-drought recovery capacities between dry and wet conditions (Vicente-Serrano et al., 2013). Furthermore, the 12-month timescale SPEI in December was indicative of the annual water availability (water balance), which could be used to quantify the impact of hydrological conditions on these cumulative and time-lag effects (Peng et al., 2019; Wei et al., 2022a; Zhao et al., 2020). Therefore, to identify the underlying influence of hydrological conditions on temporal effects, we evaluated the correlation between mean values of _R culj_ ( _R lagi_ ) and corresponding accumulated (lagged) months to annual SPEI in various climate zones and vegetation types within the YRB.

#### _3.1. Cumulative effects of drought on vegetation dynamics_ 

#### _3.1.1. Temporal variation of cumulative effects_ 

We quantified the cumulative effects of drought on the NDVI in the YRB based on the _R culj_ between the NDVI and the one- to 12-month SPEI for the period 1982 to 2015. The months of accumulated effects corresponded to the maximum _R culj_ ( _R_ max cul<sup>), indicating drought</sup> tolerance, with longer accumulation reflecting greater tolerance. Drought tolerance in the YRB varied considerably across vegetation types and climatic zones (Fig. 3). The mean _R_ max cul<sup>and vegetated areas</sup> significantly affected by the accumulated SPEI in the arid zone increased with months of accumulated effects, peaking at seven months before gradually decreasing [Fig. 3(a)]. A similar temporal variation in _R_ max cul was recorded in the semi-arid zone in comparison to the arid area, but the peak was observed in the accumulation period of five months. In contrast, two peaks of _R_ max cul<sup>occurred in the semi-humid zone, spe-</sup> cifically at months 2 and 8 of accumulated effects, respectively. In general, the _R_ max cul<sup>values and significantly correlated areas were</sup> ranked in the order arid zone _>_ semi-humid zone _>_ semi-arid zone, independent of month of accumulated effects. 

Furthermore, the vegetated areas significantly affected by accumulated drought fluctuated across vegetation types, illustrating variability in drought resilience [Fig. 3(b)]. Alpine vegetation and meadows were the least tolerant to drought, as these vegetated areas were affected by simultaneous SPEI, and the corresponding _R_ max cul<sup>values were greater</sup> than those affected by cumulative drought. On the whole, grasslands and cultivated vegetation responded most strongly to short- to mediumterm cumulative drought (accumulated effects over four–six months), whereas shrubs, conifers, and broadleaf forests were most severely affected by medium- to long-term cumulative drought (accumulated effects over seven–nine months).

#### _3.2.1. Temporal variation of time-lag effects_ 

As indicated by the _R_ max lag<sup>, the month of lagged effects corre-</sup> sponding to the highest correlation coefficient pointed to vegetation sensitivity to drought. The shorter the lagged month, the stronger the sensitivity of vegetation. As illustrated in Fig. 6(a), the extent and intensity of the effects of lagged drought on vegetation varied across climatic zones. In general, the accumulated effects of drought on vegetation in the arid, semi-arid, and semi-humid zones of the YRB occurred primarily at six to eight, four to six, and seven to eight months, respectively [Fig. 3(a)]. In arid zones, vegetation suffers from long-term water shortage, which is the key constraint on vegetation growth; such growth is facilitated by increased precipitation (soil moisture) over a given period (Fang et al., 2019a; Xu et al., 2018). The physiological characteristics and functional strategies of vegetation in arid zones for coping with water deficits have evolved, enabling allowing them to respond rapidly to variations in water availability; these characteristics and strategies include the reduction of water loss, respiratory depletion, and photosynthesis (Chaves et al., 2002; Peguero-Pina et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2020). These conditions differ from those in semi-humid regions, where soil moisture is relatively abundant and atmospheric moisture deficits are transmitted to soil over a long time, thereby diminishing vegetation tolerance for drought (Cheng et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2021). 

Furthermore, the cumulative effects of drought on vegetation in the YRB varied across vegetation types. Overall, vegetation tolerance for drought was ranked as follows: grasslands, cultivated lands, and forests—a trend that may be relevant to root characteristics and water use patterns (Ma et al., 2018; Peng et al., 2019). As a deep-rooted species, the forests in the YRB tended to tap soil water in deeper layers to alleviate water deficits under extreme droughts (Andrade et al., 2005; 

Davidson et al., 2000). Frequently, atmospheric drought initially led to a deficit in the soil water content of the surface layer, which was then gradually transferred to deeper soil layers, thereby prolonging the months of accumulated drought (Fan et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2018). Conversely, short roots enabled grasses to capture water exclusively from the surface and mid-soil layers and thus reacted quickly to atmospheric water deficits (Dodd et al., 1998; Knapp et al., 2015). Although cultivated vegetation was a shallow-rooted species, it exhibited longer months of lagged drought effects than those shown by grasslands, which may be explained by irrigation that weakens drought sensitivity (Deng et al., 2006). On this basis, there may be a connection between vegetation sensitivity to drought and anthropogenic activities, such as the breeding of drought-resistant crops and the implementation of irrigation measures (Fang et al., 2019a; Xu et al., 2018). Specifically, significant negative correlations were found between the NDVI and simultaneous SPEI in alpine vegetation and meadows, demonstrating that vegetation in these regions was highly susceptible to climate change (Ding et al., 2020; Verrall and Pickering, 2020). As these regions were characterized by high altitudes and low temperatures, excessive precipitation would have decreased temperature and solar radiation, as well as possibly caused frost damage, severely limiting vegetation growth (Ge et al., 2021; Zhan et al., 2022).

#### _4.2. Lagged effects of drought on vegetation_ 

#### **5. Conclusion** 

Although cumulative drought may have a legacy effect on vegetation, we characterized the sensitivity of vegetation to drought through correlations between NDVI and the one-month timescale SPEI. This was because to determine the sensitivity of vegetation to drought as quickly as possible, we must capture each of the fastest occurring droughts (i.e. the droughts occurring on the shortest timescale). As the SPEI described droughts based on monthly water balances (precipitation versus evapotranspiration), its shortest timescale was one month. Additionally, cumulative droughts developed from droughts that occurred on a onemonth timescale, while the occurrence of a monthly drought did not necessarily indicate a cumulative drought. Consequently, a cumulative SPEI may obscure the fact that a drought occurred over one month. We therefore examined the lagged effects of drought on vegetation using a one-month timescale SPEI. 

We found that lagged drought affected more than 90 % of the vegetated areas in the YRB and that the effects were temporal and spatially heterogeneous (Figs. 6 and 7). Consequently, water availability at a previous stage could have affected vegetation growth at the present (Ji and Peters, 2003; Peng et al., 2019). Altogether, the lagged effects of drought on vegetation in the arid, semi-arid, and semi-humid zones occurred primarily in the short (one–three months), medium (five–seven months), and long (10–12 months) terms, respectively [Fig. 6(a)]. In addition, grasslands, cultivated vegetation, and forests were sensitive to short- (one–three months), medium- (five–seven months), and long-term (10–12 months) lagged droughts, respectively. Although the temporal patterns of these lag effects in different climatic conditions and biomes were similar to the cumulative effects, the time-lag effects exhibited stronger temporal fluctuations (Wu et al., 2018). Perhaps in the process of transmission, the lagged effects of drought may have been affected by the sensitivity of species to drought, as well as local environmental changes (Anderson et al., 2010; Du et al., 2014; Wu et al., 2015). The cumulative effects were stronger than the lagged effects in most areas of the YRB, independent of the climate zone and vegetation type. This result implies that cumulative water availability in a given period had a greater impact on vegetation growth than availability at individual time points (Wei et al., 2022b). 

A lagged effect of drought on vegetation was similar to a cumulative effect under hydrological conditions. There was a decrease in the intensity of NDVI responses to annual SPEI changes as the climate became wetter, indicating that vegetation in the arid zone was more sensitive to droughts (Vicente-Serrano et al., 2013). Additionally, the NDVI tended to be affected by drought in longer months of accumulated events in the humid zone because short-term drought did not readily produce changes in the NDVI under a long-term water surplus (Fang et al., 2019a).

Although our study has provided insight into the differential 

This study analyzed the spatiotemporal patterns of drought-induced cumulative and time-lag effects on vegetation across different climatic zones and vegetation types in the YRB using the monthly NDVI, onemonth SPEI timescale (reflecting lagged drought), and one- to 12month SPEI timescale (reflecting accumulated drought). According to the maximum Pearson correlation coefficients between current monthly NDVI and accumulated (lagged) SPEI and their corresponding months of accumulated (lagged) effects, we shed light on the sensitivity to and tolerance for drought of vegetation in the region of interest. We found that vegetation growth in most of the YRB (77.4 %) depended on water resources, with the effects on 40.4 % of vegetated areas significantly facilitated by the increase in accumulated SPEI. The longest duration of cumulative effects was identified in the semi-humid zone (eight months), followed by the arid zone (seven months) and semi-arid zone (five months). In terms of main vegetation types, forests exhibited the strongest drought tolerance, followed by cultivated croplands and grasslands, whereas alpine vegetation showed the weakest drought tolerance. Vegetated areas in 91.8 % of the YRB were affected by lagged drought, with 60.6 % of the effects being significant. The arid, semi-arid, and semi-humid zones were more sensitive to short- (one–three months), medium- (five–seven months), and long-term (10–12 months) lagged drought, respectively, and grasslands were more sensitive to drought than were cultivated vegetation and forests. Annual water availability in the YRB significantly influenced the relationship between vegetation and drought, with the sensitivity to and tolerance of vegetation for accumulated (lagged) drought weakening with increasing water availability. In general, the intensity of the cumulative effects and the corresponding months of optimal response was stronger than that of the lagged effects, regardless of climatic condition and vegetation type.
