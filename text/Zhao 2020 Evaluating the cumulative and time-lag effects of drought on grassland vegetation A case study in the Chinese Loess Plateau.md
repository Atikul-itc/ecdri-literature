##### **1. Introduction** 

Global climate change is projected to increase the frequency and intensity of drought which occur under warmer conditions (Li et al., 2019a; Wang et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2019). Drought conditions are capable of influencing the normal growth of vegetation and continued drought can even trigger large-scale landscape change through causing vegetation mortality from water stress (Chu et al., 2019; Haslem et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2018a). Drought conditions are also one of the main drivers of reductions in net primary production (Zhao and Running, 2010; Zhao and Running, 2011). Numerous studies have investigated the relationship between drought and vegetation growth (Gouveia et al., 2017; Hua et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2018b). For example, Vicente-Serrano et al. (2013) evaluated the correlation between Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI) and global land biome by seasonal maximum Pearson correlation, 

and showed that more than 70% of global land vegetation has been affected by drought events. Zhang et al. (2017a) evaluated the correlation coefficient between Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and SPEI by annual maximum Pearson correlation, and found that there was a significantly positive correlation with drought effects on vegetation in most regions of China _._ The effect of drought accompanied by warmer temperature might be expected to produce even greater impacts on vegetation in the future (Cooley et al., 2019). 

Understanding how vegetation responds to drought is challenging, as the visible symptoms of drought-related effects on vegetation growth are not immediately evident. Vegetation growth may not primarily be driven by the present water condition, but by early drought events (D’Orangeville et al., 2018; Peng et al., 2019). After a severe drought, it often takes time for plant to repair the damaged root system and regain the growth capacity it had prior to the drought (Anderegg et al., 2012). Knowing the variability of the plant’s response time to drought is critical for efficient management and conservation of vegetation as well as understanding the mechanism of climate-vegetation interactions (Wen et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2015; Zhao et al., 2018a). 

An increasing number of studies have examined the time-lag effect of drought events on vegetation growth (Huang et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2015, Wu et al., 2018b). They have shown that drought has a significant time-lag effect on vegetation growth, which can vary among different species and across different ranges (Braswell, 1997; Peng et al., 2019; Vicente-Serrano et al., 2013). Despite the contributions these studies have made in explaining the interactions between drought and vegetation, they have not yet been able to integrate the magnitude and duration of drought (Hoover and Rogers., 2016). Studies on the time-lag effects of drought often focus on only a single point of time, while ignore the water conditions from that point to time of the observation, increasing large uncertainties in examining the variability of the response time drought. Incremental water deficit has been reported to be related to vegetation greenness (Berry and Mackey., 2018; M�endez-Barroso et al., 2009; Xu et al., 2011). Events such as cumulative water deficits can enhance the effects of drought on ecosystems (Ivits et al., 2016; Peng et al., 2019). Time-lag effects indicate the impacts of former drought events happened at a specific point of time on the current vegetation growth, while cumulative effects are based on the time-lag effects and include the dynamics of climatic conditions (e.g., precipitation and evapotranspiration) for a given period of time. Knowing both the time-lag effects and cumulative effects is important for understanding the interactions between drought events and vegetation growth, and also to exert more efficient vegetation management strategies. 

The Chinese Loess Plateau (CLP) is located in arid and semiarid regions, and is the one of the most severe areas of soil and water loss in the world (Shi and Shao., 2000; Zhao et al., 2013). Unfortunately, this region is experiencing an increasing tendency towards droughts both duration and severity (Wu et al., 2018a). Grassland is one of the most important ecosystems, as well as plays the key role in the soil and water conservation (Zhou et al., 2010). It is very suitable for grassland growth 

in terms of climate and soil conditions in the CLP and more than 40% of the area is covered by grassland Zhang et al., 2017b; Zheng et al., 2019). However, few studies have examined the impact of cumulative and time-lag effects of drought on grassland vegetation in the CLP. 

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the cumulative and time-lag effects of drought on grass growth in the CLP using time-series NDVI data and the SPEI data. Specifically, we aimed to assess the cumulative and time-lag effects of drought on grassland NDVI in the CLP, and to understand how these effects change with the water balance gradients. 

##### **2. Materials and methods** 

##### _2.1. Study area_ 

The CLP (102<sup>�</sup> 54<sup>0</sup> - 114<sup>�</sup> 33<sup>0</sup> E, 33<sup>�</sup> 43<sup>0</sup> - 41<sup>�</sup> 16<sup>0</sup> N) is located in north central China. It is bordered by the Taihang Mountains to the east, the Riyue Mountains to the west, the Yinshan Mountains to the north, and the Qinling Mountains to the south and covers a total area of 6.49 � 10<sup>5</sup> km<sup>2</sup> (Wang et al., 2012). The climate is typical of the continental monsoon climate zone, and the zonal distribution of climate conditions from the northwest to the southeast is from arid, to semiarid, to semi-humid (Fig. 1). The mean annual precipitation ranges from 100 mm in the northwest to 750 mm in the southeast, and the annual air temperature ranges from 4.3<sup>�</sup> C in the northwest and gradually increases southward to 14.3<sup>�</sup> C (Zhao et al., 2019). As one of the earliest human settlements, this region is also of great importance for ecological security and natural resources provisions (Zhao et al., 2013). Severe soil and water loss have led to widespread land degradation in this region; during the past decades, great efforts have been made in vegetation restoration to reduce soil erosion and water loss. About 3766.8 km<sup>2</sup> croplands were replaced by grasslands during 2000–2010 (Zhang et al., 2016b). With global warming and increased human activity, the frequency and severity of droughts has gradually increased, which has important influence on vegetation in the area (Zhang et al., 2016a). Examining how the newly established vegetation responds to drought in the CLP is urgent for long-term water and soil conservation, as well as to support the development of agriculture.

##### _2.2. Data_ 

Land cover and long-term NDVI data was used to extract the timeseries of grassland and SPEI data to assess drought severity. All the spatial datasets used in the study were resampled using a bilinear interpolation algorithm to achieve a final spatial resolution of 8 km. 

##### _2.2.1. NDVI time series_ 

The Global Inventory Monitoring and Modelling Systems (GIMMS3g) NDVI dataset was obtained from the National Oceanographic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) with an Advanced Very High Resolution Radiometer (AVHRR) to evaluate the variations of grassland vegetation in the CLP (https://ecocast.arc.nasa.gov/data/pub/gimm s/3g.v1/). This dataset is the longest record of vegetation information, spanning from 1982 to 2015 at a spatial resolution of approximately 8 km � 8 km within a 15-day interval, and has been extensively utilized to monitor vegetation dynamics (Chu et al., 2019; Kong et al., 2018; Li et al., 2019b; Na et al., 2018; Peng et al., 2019; Qu et al., 2018). The maximum value composite (MVC) technique was deployed to generate monthly NDVI. 

##### _2.2.2. Land cover data_ 

Grassland distribution in the CLP (Fig. 1) was derived from the 5.1 MODIS land cover product (MCD12Q1) collection downloaded from the USGS (United States Geological Survey) Land Cover Institute (https://la ndcover.usgs.gov/global_climatology.php) with a spatial resolution of 500 m (Broxton and Zeng, 2014). This dataset is based on the 10-year (2001–2010) land cover type data from Collection 5.1 MCD12Q1 and has been classified into 17 major land cover types (Broxton and Zeng, 2014). 

##### _2.2.3. Drought index_ 

We used the Standardized Precipitation and Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI), which is a site-specific drought indictor of deviations from the average water balance, to assess the severity and duration of droughts (Vicente-Serrano et al., 2010a, Vicente-Serrano et al., 2010b). SPEI is superior to other drought indices because it is generated by combining temperature and precipitation data (Vicente-Serrano et al., 2010a, Vicente-Serrano et al., 2010b). The monthly SPEI dataset was downloaded from SPEIbase v.2.5 at the Consejo Superior de Investigaciones Científicas (CSIC, http://digital.csic.es/handle/10261/ 153475). The database covers the period between 1901 and 2015 with a spatial resolution of 0.5<sup>�</sup> and time-scales that range from 1 to 48 months. It is evaluated based on the average water balance (precipitation minus potential evapotranspiration), where potential evapotranspiration is calculated based on the Food and Agriculture Organization � 56 Penman-Monteith method (Vicente-Serrano et al., 2010a, Vicente-Serrano et al., 2010b). For its multi-scalar essence, SPEI can represent the antecedent drought conditions as well as the accumulated values of water deficit. The accumulated water deficit at month _j_ is defined as the sum of the surplus value of SPEI of _n_ months (Peng et al., 2019). For example, the 1- month SPEI and 12-month SPEI provides the drought conditions at the current month and whole year, respectively. In this study, 1-month SPEI to 12-month SPEI datasets from 1982 to 2015 were used to assess cumulative and time-lag effects of drought on the grassland NDVI.

_3.1.2. Spatial distributions of the cumulative effects of drought on grassland NDVI_ 

Based on the correlation analysis of NDVI with accumulated SPEI, the cumulative effect of SPEI on grassland NDVI was generated using the accumulated month with the largest correlation coefficient ( _rmax-cum_ ). Overall, most grassland (80.81%) in the CLP was dominated by significant positive correlations, which were distributed in the eastern Gansu, southern Inner Mongolia, northern Shaanxi, and eastern Qinghai regions (Fig. 3a). The optimum cumulative time interval was primarily from 5 to 9 months, covering 91.95% of grassland in the CLP. In the optimum cumulative time scale, the 6-month and 8-month time scales maintained the largest grassland area (25.50% and 30.01%), including eastern Gansu, the southern Inner Mongolia, northern Shaanxi, and the southern Ningxia (Fig. 3b).

##### _3.2. Time lag effect of drought on grassland vegetation_ 

##### _3.2.1. Response of grasslands NDVI to drought by time lag scales_ 

The time lags of grassland vegetation response to drought in the CLP indicated that most of the grassland responded to drought within 2–3 months. Drought was found to have a variable time-lag effect on vegetation in the CLP. The correlation between NDVI and monthly SPEI increased rapidly from the one-month lag, and reached a peak value at the three-month lag (Fig. 5). It then declined from the lag of three to nine months, but then increased again from nine to 12 months. The NDVI of most of the areas of the CLP was significantly positively correlated with the one-, two-, and three-month SPEI, indicating a quick response of vegetation to the drought. In detail, 10.37% of the area had a significant positive correlation between NDVI and the one-month SPEI. This then increased to 35.94% with the two-month SPEI, and reached 46.12% with the three-month SPEI. These areas are mainly located in the western Inner Mongolia, eastern Ningxia, and northeast Shaanxi regions (Figs. S2a–c). 

##### _3.2.2. Spatial distributions of the time-lag effects of drought on grassland NDVI_ 

With the maximum correlation ( _rmax-lag_ ) between NDVI and onemonth SPEI (a time-lag range from 0 to 12 months), the time-lag effect was summarized for each pixel. Overall, 54.52% of the grassland presented a time-lag response to drought, distributed in the western Inner Mongolia, eastern Ningxia, and northeast Shaanxi regions (Fig. 6a). Among the area with a time-lag response, about 96.85% maintained a one-to three-month time-lag response of vegetation to drought, and the _rmax-lag_ at time-lags of two-to three-months occupied most of the area (33.35% and 56.17%; Fig. 6b). 

##### _3.2.3. Time lag effects of drought on grassland NDVI with the different water availability conditions_ 

The patterns of the lagged effect of drought on grassland NDVI with different water availability conditions were calculated (Fig. 7). Negative correlations between the mean annual SPEI (water balance gradient), mean _rmax-lag_ , and corresponding mean lagged months were found. In the view of lagged time scale, the mean lagged months indicated a decreased trend (from 3.14 to 2.86) with an increasing mean annual SPEI, indicating that drought had increased lagged effects on the grassland NDVI within longer time scales in the drier areas when compared to wetter areas ( _R_<sup>_2_</sup> ¼ 0.547, _P <_ 0.05). From the perspective of _rmax-lag_ , the mean _rmax-lag_ showed a significant negative correlation to the mean annual SPEI ( _R_<sup>_2_</sup> ¼ 0.785, _P <_ 0.01), indicating that the grassland NDVI in arid areas usually experienced a stronger time-lag effect (Fig. 7b).

##### **4. Discussion** 

##### _4.1. The cumulative effect of drought on grassland vegetation_ 

Vegetation growth is greatly impacted by water deficits. The CLP is a critical ecological constructive area where grassland is one of the most important and dominant vegetation types (Gang et al., 2018; Zheng et al., 2019). However, the CLP is suffering from severe droughts which can have significant impacts on the ecosystem within the region. Zhao 

et al. (2019) reported that 21.54% of the CLP was affected by drought. Hua et al. (2017) confirmed that vegetation activity during the growing season was highly correlated with drought occurrences in the semi-arid regions of China. The results of this study indicate that cumulative water deficits affect vegetation growth in most of the grassland (80.84%) in the CLP, over a quite long cumulative time scale of five-to nine-months (Fig. 2). In the arid and semi-arid areas, the grassland has widely developed with well-developed root systems, and as soil moisture is the primary source of water for vegetation growth (Guo and Shao, 2002), water availability at longer timescales may have a significant influence on vegetation activity (Fig. 4), which is a result supported by several previous studies (Ji and Peters., 2003; Hua et al., 2017). 

##### _4.2. The time lag effect of drought on grassland vegetation_ 

Similar to the cumulative effect, vegetation in a large proportion of the grassland in the CLP has a time-lag response to droughts (Fig. 5). Most of the grassland vegetation was found to have a one-to three-month time-lag to droughts. Similar to the time-lag effect of precipitation at the end of growing season (Cong et al., 2017; Peng et al., 2019), our study results indicated that the three-month drought lag had the strongest impact on grassland NDVI. In the arid, semiarid, and semi humid areas, the major limiting factor for vegetation growth was soil moisture, and a two-to three-month lag was found in the responses of grassland NDVI in these areas to drought. This may be due to the intrinsic physiological mechanisms of vegetation that allow the species in these areas to tolerate water deficits for longer periods of time, and similar results were also found in previous studies (Anderegg et al., 2015; Vicente-Serrano et al., 2013; Wen et al., 2019). ##### _4.3. Limitations and implications for future work_ 

Some limitations and uncertainties still exist in this study. First, NDVI and SPEI were used to analyze the responses of grasslands growth to drought, and it should be noted that the SPEI values were calculated by using the monthly precipitation and potential evapotranspiration. While the dynamics of precipitation and potential evapotranspiration are also significant characteristics of climatic condition, the impacts of their dynamics on vegetation growth can provide important insights into the mechanism of vegetation response to climate. Numerous studies have reported that increasing potential evapotranspiration seems to intensify the aridity of an environment (Cook et al., 2014). Although the precipitation and potential evapotranspiration were considered to calculate SPEI in this study, how the complex interactions between different environmental factors affect vegetation growth requires more studies. Furthermore, the correlation analysis was adopted to calculate the relationships between grassland vegetation and drought. However, the relationships between vegetation growth and drought can be nonlinear due to their complicated interactions involved. Therefore, using simple correlation analysis may generate oversimplified results about the cumulative and time-lag effects of vegetation’s response to drought (Liu et al., 2015; Wen et al., 2019). Lastly, this study focused only the responses of vegetation to drought, while the impacts from human activities (such as ecological recovery programs, urban sprawl and agricultural irrigation), other no-climatic factors (such as CO2 fertilization, nitrogen and phosphorus deposition), and natural disturbances (such as wildfires, plant diseases and insect pests) can also have unneglectable impacts on vegetation growth (Flower and Gonzalez-Meler, 2015; Huang et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2017). Efficient management of vegetation would be facilitated by comprehensive understanding of how vegetation responds to different types of disturbance. 

##### **5. Conclusions** 

In the present study, using the vegetation index (NDVI) and monthly SPEI, we assessed the cumulative and time-lag effects of drought on grassland growth over in the period of 1982–2015. The key results show that: antecedent water deficits cumulatively impact vegetation 

variations; drought had a quite long cumulative effect (5- to 10- month) on vegetation growth in most of the grassland of the CLP. The cumulative effects of drought on grassland NDVI were presented in 80.81% of the grassland in the CLP, mostly located in the eastern Gansu, southern Inner Mongolia, northern Shaanxi, and southern Ningxia regions. Vegetation in arid areas was found to experience stronger cumulative effects of drought which were characterized by a longer cumulative time scale, and the time-lag effect of drought on vegetation growth in the study area mainly happens in one to three months, with the three-month lag having the strongest effect on grassland vegetation. Vegetation in about half of the grassland in the CLP was found to have experienced a time-lag effect of drought, primarily in areas located in the western Inner Mongolia, eastern Ningxia, and northeast Shaanxi regions. The lagged months and mean _r_ max __cum_ had a negative correlation with the mean annual SPEI, respectively ( _R_<sup>_2_</sup> ¼ 0.547, _P <_ 0.05; _R_<sup>_2_</sup> ¼ 0.785, _P <_ 0.01). Overall, the drought events had significant cumulative and timelag effect on grassland growth, this study provides a comprehensive understanding of the cumulative and time-lag effects of drought on vegetation growth in the grassland of the CLP, and the results hold significant value for vegetation conservation strategies, response of vegetation to climate change, and water and soil restoration in the CLP. Our study highlights the importance of both cumulative and lag effect of drought on vegetation. Due to vegetation’s different lag and cumulative response to drought, planners should consider what type of vegetation to plant and how to conserve existing vegetation.
