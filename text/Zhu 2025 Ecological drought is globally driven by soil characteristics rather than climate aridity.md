## Ecological drought is globally driven by soil characteristics rather than climate aridity 



Xinrong Zhu<sup>a</sup> , Lu Wang<sup>b</sup> , Liang Shi<sup>c</sup> , Lu Wu<sup>d</sup> , Boyi Liang<sup>e</sup> , Hongyan Liu<sup>a,*</sup>

A R T I C L E I N F O _Keywords:_ Layered soil water use Climate Soil texture Root depth Soil water distribution 

A B S T R A C T 

Although the distribution of vegetation growth is largely determined by soil moisture availability, what determines the use of soil water at different depths by vegetation still requires investigation. Here, we used remote sensing proxy of global vegetation growth and soil moisture data to link gridded layered soil water use patterns with climate aridity, soil texture, root depth and soil water distribution characteristics. We found no significant differences in the soil water use patterns between the climate subtypes. Soil texture can explain the divergence in soil water dependence between the surface and subsurface soil layers by regulating both the layered soil water distribution and root water uptake depth. However, the controlling effect of a coarser soil texture to enhance deepwater uptake is limited in water-limited regions. A structural equation model reveals the mechanisms of vegetation growth dependence on surface and subsurface soil moisture globally. Layered soil water distribution regulated by soil texture can determine whether vegetation water stress is mostly dominated by the variation in surface or subsurface soil moisture supply across climates, and this effect peaks in global semiarid regions. Our results suggested that soil characteristics, rather than climate aridity, dominantly regulate global soil water use for vegetation growth, which is crucial for understanding the occurrence of ecological drought as a new circumstance in simulations of global change. 

#### **1. Introduction** 

As water availability is fundamental for plant growth, terrestrial ecosystem at the global scale is expected to become more water constrained with ongoing and warmer climate change (Jiao et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022a). Previous researchers who explored dominant hydrometeorological controls of vegetation growth have recognized that vegetation growth is significantly impacted or even dominated by soil water supply capacity across different ecosystems at local, regional and global scales compared with climatic aridity (Li et al., 2022b; Liu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023; Kannenberg et al., 2024). Similar to climatic aridity, soil moisture has horizontal heterogeneity and temporal variability, but the greatest difference from climatic aridity is that soil moisture is also characterized by interlayer variability (Li et al., 2017; Jia et al., 2020; Datta and Faroughi, 2023), as unpredictable rainfall pulses need to be translated into usable soil moisture pulses in soil layers to support 

vegetation growth (Crausbay et al., 2017; Slette et al., 2019; Crausbay et al., 2020). The question of particular interest is whether separation of soil water into different layers with depth occurs to support vegetation growth and its determinants. Therefore, defining and quantifying the layered soil water use pattern is essential for advancing our understanding and prediction of ecological drought. 

From the perspective of plant soil water use, a more accurate representation of the vegetation response to soil water stress requires the differentiation between soil layers. Plant water uptake often occurs in two soil layers, one in shallow soil, where water is available in pulses, and the other in deeper soil, where water availability is assumed to be more constant (Schwinning and Ehleringer, 2001; Zhu et al., 2016). Currently, soil moisture datasets at the regional and global scales are available both spatially and temporally, as global observations of surface soil moisture are now available from Earth-orbiting satellites (McColl et al., 2017; Feldman et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). At regional and global scales, however, climate aridity, soil texture, root depth and soil water distribution interact with each other, so how these characteristics jointly determine layered soil water use patterns for global vegetation remains to be tested (Fig. 1). 

In this study, we depicted the relationships between layered soil moisture and vegetation growth using a remote sensing proxy of global vegetation growth (i.e., NDVI) and soil moisture data to advance the understanding of the layered soil water use pattern globally. We also investigated how this soil water use pattern is modulated by soil layers with depth-varying hydrological properties by establishing a structural equation model to link climate aridity, soil texture, root depth and soil water distribution, which can reveal the critical paths regulating ecological drought sensitivity across soil layers. We aimed to obtain a more comprehensive assessment of soil moisture effects across layers on vegetation growth, which contributes to understanding the determinants and mechanisms of layered soil moisture availability for vegetation growth as a new focus of ecological drought research. 

#### **2. Materials and methods** 

#### _2.1. Climate, soil and vegetation dataset_ 

The data used in this study were openly available in the following databases. The normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) dataset was obtained from third-generation biweekly Advanced Very High Resolution Radiometer (AVHRR) data from the Global Inventory Modeling and Mapping Studies (GIMMS) project as a proxy for vegetation growth between 1982 and 2015 (Tian et al., 2015). The global soil texture data were extracted from version 1.2 of the Harmonized World Soil Database (HWSD) (available at http://www.fao.org/soils 

-portal/soil-survey/soil-maps-and-databases/harmo-nized-world-soil 

-dadatabase-v12/en/), which represents the most comprehensive soil database to date. The dataset combined existing regional and national updates of soil information worldwide and provided global soil texture at a resolution of 30 arc-seconds based on the U.S. classification standard for sand, silt, and clay fractions (Kochy et al., 2015). The global dataset of necessary root-water uptake depths provided an estimated maximum depth of root uptake averaged over ten years (2004–2013) with global grids of 1 km (available at https://wci.earth2observe. 

eu/thredds/catalog/usc/root-depth/catalog.html) (Fan et al., 2017). The land cover data come from the EOS/MODIS data of NASA, available at https://lpdaac.usgs.gov/products/mcd12q1v061/. In this study, the land cover maps were reclassified, and three dominant categories (water bodies, cropland, urban and built-up land) were eliminated based on the original 17 land use types of the International Geosphere-Biosphere Program classification scheme. Climate aridity was quantified by the most widely used aridity index (AI), which represents the balance between precipitation supply and atmospheric water demand (potential evapotranspiration, PET). The generalized climate classification schemes for the aridity index values are 0.2, 0.5 and 0.65, corresponding to the thresholds for global arid, semiarid and subhumid regions, respectively (Zomer et al., 2022).

#### _2.3. Defining soil water use patterns by the effects of soil moisture across_ 

#### _layers on vegetation growth_ 

In this step, we aimed to infer layered soil moisture availability to support vegetation growth by modeling the relationship between 

vegetation growth and layered soil moisture use. First, to remove the interference of temperature fluctuation effects on vegetation growth, we fitted a linear regression between temperature and NDVI anomalies in each grid and then used the fitted regression to remove the temperaturedriven component (whether positive or negative anomalies) from the NDVI values. Then, we proposed a preset pattern of vegetation growth supported by the water supply from two soil layers (surface and subsurface) and established the relationships between the calibrated NDVI (after removing the temperature impacts) and the layered soil moisture in each grid. As with all analyses of global soil moisture availability for vegetation growth, the covariance of surface soil moisture and subsurface soil moisture is an important consideration, potentially affecting the specific model parameter estimates; thus, these findings should be interpreted with caution. To address this issue, we used a stepwise multiple mixed regression model to select the most important candidate variables (surface or subsurface soil moisture) for explaining the layered soil moisture availability by minimizing the Akaike information criterion (Maire et al., 2015). In this way, the global partitioning of soil moisture-related controls on vegetation growth significantly varied among the three experiments involving the surface layer, subsurface layer or both layers.

#### _2.4. Establishment of a potential mechanism that determines soil water_ 

_use patterns_ 

There is limited opportunity to test soil effects on varied vegetation dependence on surface or subsurface soil moisture across a wider diversity of biomes and climates, especially at spatiotemporal scales of interest to Earth system models. To further explore whether, as we hypothesized, there was a potential relationship between soil layer characteristics and layered soil moisture availability for vegetation growth at a global scale, we used a structural equation model (SEM) to examine the direct and latent relationships between variables, including climate aridity, soil texture, root depth and soil water distribution, that jointly determine layered soil water use patterns for global vegetation. Soil water use patterns revealing water stress from the surface or subsurface soil layer can be designed as a conceptual function involving multiple regulating pathways that occur in soil layers, four direct pathways of climate aridity, soil texture and root-water uptake depth and layered soil water distribution, and many potential indirect pathways, including the indirect effects of soil texture by impacting soil water distribution and root depth, the indirect effects of climate on soil texture and root depth and the effects of root-water uptake depth on soil water distribution. 

The aridity index was selected as a climate aridity indicator. For soil texture, we extracted the sand/silt ratio to characterize soil coarsening. ANOVA was also used to determine the differences in soil texture characteristics under the different soil water use patterns. In this study, we also used the soil moisture difference between the surface and subsurface to comprehensively characterize the soil water supply across layers after redistribution (i.e., the layered SM distribution via SEM). 

The selected relevant indicators and the quantified soil water use patterns were adopted to construct the structural equation model (SEM). A reasonable SEM should start with a conceptual model with all possible paths and then use a stepwise procedure to remove the paths that did not contribute to the model fit, which can be inferred by the parameters. All plausible interaction pathways derived directly from our theory are shown in the SEMs (Fig. 2). Then, we sequentially eliminated nonsignificant pathways to obtain the parsimonious set of models. Removing those nonsignificant pathways did not improve the model or reduce the goodness of fit. Moreover, the obtained structural equation models based on our hypothesis met the following criteria: the p values of χ2 and the goodness of fit test p _>_ 0.05, the comparative fit index _>_ 0.9, and the root mean square error of approximation _<_ 0.05, which all indicated that the model had an excellent fit (Rosseel, 2012). An excellent-fitting SEM helps to further clarify the soil water use regime for vegetation while accounting for the role of climate, soil texture, root depth and soil water distribution across layers. 

#### **3. Results** 

#### _3.1. Global soil water use patterns for vegetation growth_ 

Only 15 % of the grids followed a significant soil moisture use pattern: either surface or subsurface soil moisture controlled vegetation growth, accounting for 7 % and 8 %, respectively, and more than 54 % of global terrestrial ecosystems were supported by both surface and 

subsurface soil water supplies. Other areas (approximately 16 %) were farmland areas, urban coverage areas and missing data areas that were excluded from the results to reduce interference from land cover data. There are also some places accounting for approximately 13 % where our method does not work and that do not show clear patterns of vegetation soil water use. They are light yellow, and the value is close to 0 (Fig. 3). These grids are mainly concentrated in tropical and subtropical moist forests, including those in the Amazon region, central Africa and southern China. 

The change in climatic aridity did not determine whether vegetation growth was dominated by water from the surface or the subsurface soil layer. Specifically, the subsurface soil water supply capacity dominated vegetation growth in water-limited and humid regions, accounting for 58 % and 60 % of areas experienced subsurface drought stress, respectively. The highest subsoil moisture dependence, indicating a dominant stress from dryness of the subsurface soil layer, limits vegetation growth and is mostly concentrated in southwestern North America, southern Africa and eastern Australia. Moreover, the effects of surface soil moisture on vegetation growth are much smaller in these areas. However, in northern Africa, western Asia and northern China, our result shows that given lower variability of deeper soil moisture in these areas, the survival of vegetation is more reliant on surface soil moisture availability rather than subsurface soil moisture variation. 

#### _3.2. Soil texture and water supply under different soil water use patterns_ 

Taking climate aridity as the dividing line, the soil texture characteristics under the different soil water use patterns were calculated separately. The soil texture characteristics were quantified by the ratio of the sand/silt fraction; the higher the score of the ratio fraction was, the coarser the soil texture. There was no significant difference in the sand/silt fraction between water-limited and humid ecosystems. Moreover, the soil texture significantly differed under the different soil water use patterns. In both water-limited regions and humid regions, a coarser soil texture tended to make vegetation growth more dependent on subsurface soil moisture (Fig. 4a). 

Based on the plausible pathways proposed by our previous assumption, we further examined whether soil water is separated into different layers with depth to support vegetation growth by establishing the relationships between layered soil water distribution and vegetation soil water use patterns. The difference in the soil water distribution characteristics explained the differences in the vegetation growth dependence on surface and subsurface soil moisture. A greater dependence on subsurface soil moisture for vegetation growth corresponded to higher subsurface soil moisture and a lower coefficient of variation (CV) of soil moisture change relative to the surface soil water supply (Fig. 4b). 

#### _3.3. Paths regulating layered soil water use patterns by vegetation_ 

There was a separation of soil water into different layers with depth to support vegetation growth, but the effects of climate aridity, soil texture and root-water uptake depth on vegetation soil water use patterns were achieved via different pathways. There was a direct significant positive effect of an increased sand/silt ratio, resulting in a greater soil moisture supply in the subsurface soil layer than in the surface soil layer, further increasing the dependence of vegetation growth on subsurface soil moisture, which was revealed by the significant correlations in the SEM (Fig. 5a). The relationships between climate aridity and soil water use patterns were mainly connected by affecting the root depth distribution, revealing that increased climate aridity encouraged a deep root depth. However, the root depth was not only regulated by climate but also regulated by soil texture and affected the layered soil water distribution, showing that coarse-textured soil not only enhanced deepwater uptake by roots but also contributed to a higher and more stable subsurface soil water supply relative to the surface soil water supply. #### **4. Discussion and conclusion** 

In this study, we redefined the soil water use pattern for vegetation growth by quantifying vegetation dependence on layered soil moisture. We distinguished the two major ecosystem-level soil moisture use patterns for vegetation growth from previous studies (Reynolds et al., 2004; Luo et al., 2022; Li et al., 2022b), depending on surface soil moisture or subsurface soil moisture, using full satellite records of long-term vegetation and soil moisture satellite data. In contrast to previous studies in which vegetation growth in water-limited regions was supported by a shallow water pool in the surface soil layer and vegetation growth in humid regions was supported by a more constant deepwater pool in the subsoil layer (Schwinning, 2010; Wang et al., 2021), our results showed that the change in climatic aridity does not determine whether vegetation growth is dominated by water from the surface or subsurface soil layer. Moreover, we also found our method failed to show clear patterns in soil water use pattern for vegetation in tropical or subtropical moist forests, either because of limitations with the GLEAM dataset or because of fundamental difficulties in measuring vegetation dynamics using the NDVI in tropical or subtropical moist forests (Camps-Valls et al., 2021). 

Sand, clay and silt fractions were used to examine their effects on soil moisture availability for vegetation growth, but the related findings were highly contradictory. Previous studies have highlighted the local relevance of the silt fraction to ecosystems, as silt adjusts the water holding capacity of soil, and the unit cost of water for vegetation growth decreases as the silt content of soil increases (Paillassa et al., 2020), while other studies stress the importance of the sand fraction to increase the soil moisture availability for vegetation growth by facilitating infiltration and percolation into deeper soil layers and reducing bare soil evaporation (Levine et al., 2016; Zhu et al., 2022). Our results showed that an increased sand/silt ratio contributes to subsurface soil moisture availability for vegetation growth at the global scale, regardless of climate aridity. The effect on soil texture effect caused by impacting soil water distribution is stable under various climate aridity gradients, but the effect of soil texture caused by impacting root distribution becomes weaker or disappears with the release of climate drought stress, which is also supported by observed results from site-scale or regional-scale 

studies (Renne et al., 2019; Jiang et al., 2022). Our results revealed critical pathways regulating layered soil water use in soil and provided a missing empirical baseline for divergent ecological drought sensitivities across soil layers. 

The main source of uncertainty in this study might come from the satellite-based products used in the analysis regarding their retrieval uncertainties, especially for subsoil moisture, as this layer does not have direct satellite data for assimilation. We reduced the impact of dataset uncertainty by selecting a dataset derived semiempirically and independent of assimilated reanalysis products that applied process-based models. We further note that other water sources, such as bedrock water and groundwater, also impact vegetation productivity, but we did not explore these sources in this study (McCormick et al., 2021; MiguezMacho and Fan, 2021). Previous studies have shown that vegetation responses to deep soil water sources, including groundwater, can also be largely captured by subsurface moisture dynamics in many regions (Wahr et al., 2004; Li et al., 2022c). Therefore, if vegetation growth is dependent on deeper soil water sources, it is also most likely to be more dependent on the water supply from the subsurface soil layer than on that from the surface layer. Although we focused only on the comparison of surface and subsurface layers when defining soil water use patterns, these omissions did not have a subversive impact on our conclusions. 

Despite these uncertainties that cannot be eliminated, our results revealed that the combined impacts of climate, soil texture and root depth lead to divergent soil moisture accessibility and availability for vegetation growth and provided evidence to support the dominant role of soil in determining the soil water use regime. More explicitly, assessing the critical paths that occur in soil layers to determine layered soil water use for vegetation growth at a global scale in the future will benefit a full and detailed understanding of water use and constraints in terrestrial ecosystems.
